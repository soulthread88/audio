[p01] Brussels, Belgium · NATO emergency video conference, March 16, 9:12 AM
Day 17 of the war. The United States formally asked its allies for military help to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. France refused first. Germany, Italy, Spain and Belgium followed. China refused too. Within two hours the call was over, and President Trump issued a sharply worded statement condemning the allies.

[p02] Seoul, South Korea · Ministry of Foreign Affairs, March 16, 8:30 AM
South Korea, Japan and Australia also declined to join the Hormuz coalition. All three cited constitutional limits on sending forces abroad without parliamentary approval. Seoul pledged only $500,000 in humanitarian aid and position data for Korean-flagged ships. Trump answered the allies with a declaration: America would win alone.

[p03] North Tehran · Shahrak-e Gharb, March 17, 3:18 AM
Day 18, before dawn. With U.S. cooperation, Israel struck several senior Iranian officials at once. At 5:12 AM, Iranian state TV confirmed the deaths of former parliament speaker Ali Larijani and Basij commander Gholamreza Soleimani. The oldest channel in Iran's nuclear diplomacy had closed.

[p04] Ramat Gan, Israel · Southern Lebanon border, March 17
Iran's retaliation began one hour and forty-two minutes later. Ballistic missiles hit central Ramat Gan, killing two Israeli civilians. That afternoon, Israel launched a ground invasion of southern Lebanon. UNICEF announced that more than 1,100 children had been killed or wounded since the war began.

[p05] Washington, D.C. · White House Situation Room — The Moment No One Stands
There were fourteen monitors, and every one of them turned red at once. National Security Adviser Brian Collins did not get up from his chair. The most dangerous moment in a war isn't when everyone jumps to their feet. It's when no one does. Confusion makes people move. Panic makes them go still.
“NATO's out, sir. Japan too. Korea. Australia.” His aide kept his voice low. Collins didn't even nod. An alliance was never a person. An alliance was a system, and systems always flow toward their own interests. A war you start alone, you finish alone.

[p06] Seoul · The Foreign Minister's Office — A Two-Line Cable
Foreign Minister Lee Junhyeok stood at the window, looking down at Seoul. People were walking to work, knowing nothing. He envied them. The cable from the U.S. Embassy was two lines long. A request to join the Hormuz operation. An answer required within twenty-four hours.
The reply had already gone out under his name. Formal regrets, constitutional constraints, a strong call for diplomacy. “Tell them there's no official reaction. We support it, but we won't take part. That's all.” When one nation leaves another alone on the battlefield, it is always done in a properly formatted document.

[p07] Kuwait City · Salmiya District — A Night Without Power
The children were asleep. Fatima al-Rashid had put all three to bed and sat down in the living room. Since the war began, every sound had changed. An engine sounded like a missile. Lightning felt like an explosion. That night, an Iranian drone struck an eight-story apartment building in Salmiya.
The ceiling shook. Her youngest was crying — five years old, not even sure why. Something was frightening, and that was reason enough. Fatima held her and looked out the window. Smoke rose from the building next door. There were no lights. Wars are fought between soldiers. But in the darkness a war leaves behind, the ones who cry are always the children.

[p08] Above North Tehran · F-35I Cockpit — The Two-Finger Rule
Altitude: twelve thousand meters. The Israeli pilot checked the coordinates. Then he checked again. The target was not a soldier. But in war, the line between civilians and the powerful exists only on paper. He thought of his mother in Haifa — the woman who never wanted her son in this seat.
The release button is pressed with two fingers. That's the rule. One finger is a mistake. Two fingers make it a decision. There were two explosions. The first took off the roof. No one remembers the second. Memory only reaches as far as the moment before.

[p09] Ramat Gan, Israel · A Residential District — The Morning the War Came
The siren had sounded six minutes earlier. David Levy, seventy-two, did not go down to the shelter. He had lived through 1967, 1973, 2006. He had heard too many sirens. He stood at his window. In the early morning, Ramat Gan glowed the color of a sunset.
The glass trembled. The next moment, he was lying on the floor. The sound had gone out of his ears. The ceiling was cracked, and dust drifted down. This is war. Not because I didn't take shelter. Because the war came looking for me. Civilians never choose war. But war always chooses civilians.

[p10] Southern Lebanon · The Border Zone — A Line Being Erased
The treads of a Merkava tank were far quieter than people imagine. Silent. Ominously silent. For Mohammad Kasser at a Hezbollah lookout post, the tanks he couldn't see were the most terrifying. If you could see them, at least you knew which way to run.
This was not an air strike. Air strikes came down from the sky. This came across the ground. Armor. Infantry. Artillery. The oldest form of war. He picked up his AK-47. He couldn't shoot the sky. He couldn't stop a drone. A border is a line on a map. Tank treads roll right over it — and the line is erased.

[p11] UNICEF Headquarters, New York → Iran and Lebanon — Numbers Without Names
There was a number. More than eleven hundred. Two hundred in Iran. Ninety-one in Lebanon. Four in Israel. One in Kuwait. The report had no names in it. Only numbers. Numbers are tidy. Children's names are not.
In Isfahan, Zahra was twelve. She fell on a stairwell while running for shelter and hurt her head. It wasn't a missile. It was fear. In Nabatieh, Youssef is six. He is alive, but his right ear no longer hears. Children don't know why anyone is fighting. Adults think they do. That difference is what makes all of this.

[p12] Strait of Hormuz · USS Gerald R. Ford, CIC — Watching Is a Choice
Lieutenant Kim Junho watched the screens in the combat information center. A Korean Navy liaison officer, his job was to send Seoul what he saw and heard. Larijani's name appeared on the list of confirmed dead. “I hear Trump went after Korea too?” asked U.S. Navy officer Mark Henderson.
“No official reaction from our side.” Henderson gave a bitter laugh. “That's diplomacy. Saying nothing is still saying something.” Kim didn't nod. On a carrier before dawn, he wasn't sure saying nothing was really all right. Watching was a choice too. And every choice came with responsibility.

[p13] Nowhere — The Language of War
In Tehran, Larijani was dead. In Ramat Gan, two civilians were killed. In southern Lebanon, tanks crossed the border. In Kuwait, a child cried. In Seoul, a minister looked out a window. And millions of people, watching the news, turned off their screens — not because it was unbearable, but because they had seen too much to feel it.
Rumi's Masnavi begins: “Listen to the reed, how it tells a tale, complaining of separation.” A reed only makes a sound once it's been torn from the reed bed. Whether that sound is a melody or a scream depends on the ear that hears it. The cruelest moment of war isn't the bomb. It's when we get used to it.
